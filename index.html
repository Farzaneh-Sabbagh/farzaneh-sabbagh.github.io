About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in economics at the University of New Hampshire. I study how environmental quality and community composition shape the value people place on public goods: my dissertation asks what cleaner water in Lake Erie is worth to the people who swim and fish in it, and why some communities vote to invest in their schools while others do not. I expect to defend my dissertation in May 2027.

Before UNH, I earned an M.Sc. in Quantitative Economics at Bielefeld University in Germany, and a B.Sc. in Economics at the University of Tehran. I work in R, Stata, Python, and MATLAB, with ArcGIS and QGIS for spatial work, and I speak English, Farsi, and German.

I have also worked outside the university. As a sustainability intern at ProAmpac, a global packaging manufacturer, I analyzed energy and emissions data across its operations and presented reduction strategies to corporate leadership. At UNH’s Center for Business Analytics, I designed surveys and delivered analytics for client-facing projects.

Research

Publication

Mohr, R., & Sabbagh, F. (2026). National priorities list. In D. E. Taylor (Ed.), The Sage Encyclopedia of Environmental Justice (Vol. 3, pp. 875–880). SAGE Publications. doi.org/10.4135/9781071908006.n265

This entry reviews the National Priorities List (NPL), the roster of the most hazardous waste sites in the United States designated for long-term cleanup under the Superfund program. We describe how sites are listed and remediated, synthesize the evidence on the racial and socioeconomic disparities linked to site location and cleanup duration, and review what is known about the health, property-value, and gentrification effects of remediation on nearby communities.

Job Market Paper

“Are Nutrient Policy Impacts on Recreation in Lake Erie as Murky as the Water?” (with S. Brockmann) · supported by an external research grant from the Great Lakes Fishery Commission, 2023–2026

Harmful algal blooms have re-emerged as the central management challenge on Lake Erie, prompting the United States and Canada to commit to a 40% cut in the phosphorus loading that feeds them. Less phosphorus is not an unambiguous gain, because the lake serves two very different groups of recreational users: beachgoers, whose visits depend on water conditions, and anglers, whose visits depend on the fishery that those same nutrients help sustain. To measure how each group values the policy, I designed and administered two contingent-behavior surveys that record respondents’ actual trips to the lake alongside the trips they say they would take under hypothetical scenarios varying bloom severity, water safety, and fish populations. Using the travel cost method, I estimate negative binomial recreation demand models in which each respondent’s actual and hypothetical trips are pooled in a single estimation, so that real behavior anchors the models while the hypothetical scenarios identify responses to conditions the lake has not yet experienced. I then feed simulations from a calibrated ecosystem model of the lake into these demand functions; to my knowledge, no earlier study runs an ecosystem model’s policy simulations through a demand model that pools actual and hypothetical trips for two user groups. Beachgoers respond to bloom severity with a threshold: moderate and severe blooms deter visits almost equally, so beach-trip gains are modest under the 40% target and grow sharply only under deeper reductions that restore mild conditions. Anglers, by contrast, respond to fish abundance and water safety rather than bloom size. The paper quantifies the lake’s documented water quality–fisheries tradeoff in economic terms, delivering the distributional evidence regulators need to compare nutrient targets on a common welfare footing.

Working Paper

“How Racial Composition Shapes Local Investment in Education: Voter–Student Demographic Mismatch and School Bond Elections in Texas” (with Robert Mohr and Karen Conway)

School bonds are the primary way American communities finance long-term educational investments, yet they are decided in elections by adult voters whose racial and ethnic composition differs from that of the K–12 students the funds are intended to serve. This paper asks whether that mismatch affects bond passage. We develop a model of voting behavior in which a voter’s decision is influenced by both their own race or ethnicity and the racial and ethnic composition of the district’s student population. The model yields a measure of racial fragmentation, the Hybrid Fragmentation Index (HFI) of Kurtz et al. (2024), which is highest when the voting-age population is least representative of the student population. We construct a district-year panel of Texas school bond elections that links each district’s voting-age population to its enrolled students. In models with district and year fixed effects, greater voter–student mismatch, as measured by the HFI, significantly reduces the probability of bond passage. We also uncover a reversal overlooked in earlier studies: the effect of a district’s over-65 population on bond passage is negative early in the sample but becomes significantly more positive over time, suggesting that racial and ethnic mismatch, rather than age itself, may be a key barrier to educational investment.

Teaching

This fall I am the instructor of record for Principles of Microeconomics (ECON 402), a class of 75 students for which I designed the syllabus, lectures, assignments, and exams. As a teaching assistant at UNH, I have led the undergraduate econometrics labs for Introduction to Econometrics and Advanced Econometrics, the doctoral labs for Econometrics I (ECON 926) and Microeconomics II, and review sessions for Principles and Intermediate Microeconomics; I have been a guest lecturer in a new course on AI Prompt Engineering for Economics; and I have graded and given written feedback on analytical projects for Industrial Economics and Business Innovation, Innovation in the Global Economy, and International Economics.

In my most recent evaluated course, the Fall 2024 graduate Econometrics I lab, every student who responded rated both the course and my teaching “Recommend Strongly.” In 2025 I received UNH’s Summer Teaching Assistant Fellowship, awarded competitively on academic performance, teaching service, and faculty recommendations.

Prepared to teach: principles and intermediate microeconomics, econometrics (R or Stata), environmental and natural resource economics, public economics, cost-benefit analysis and non-market valuation, data analytics, and undergraduate international economics.
